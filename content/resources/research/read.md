# Read

## Decide the purpose before you open the file

Reading for an overview, reading in depth, hunting for prior work, evaluating whether a paper is relevant at all, and reading to understand one concept are five different tasks. They have different stopping rules. Most wasted reading time is a purpose mismatch — reading in depth something you only needed to place, or skimming something you actually needed to follow.

## The method

**[How to Read a Paper](http://svr-sk818-web.cl.cam.ac.uk/keshav/papers/07/paper-reading.pdf) — S. Keshav** (ACM SIGCOMM CCR, 2007).

Three passes.

*First pass*, five to ten minutes: title, abstract, introduction; the section and subsection headings and nothing under them; a glance at the mathematics to see what it rests on; the conclusions. At the end you can say what kind of paper it is and whether you want the second pass.

*Second pass*, up to an hour: read the body carefully but skip the proofs. Look hard at the figures — badly labelled axes and results without error bars are the fastest tell. Note the references you haven't read.

*Third pass*, hours: reconstruct the paper. Make the same assumptions and re-derive. This is where you find the unstated assumptions, the missing citation, and the experiment that would have been the honest one to run.

The same paper also gives the best short recipe for a literature survey: use the passes to find the three or four most-cited recent papers in the area, read their related-work sections, find the shared venue or the shared authors, and go there.

**Strategies for reading academic articles.** The complement to Keshav, and the same insight from the humanities side: read for the structure of the argument rather than front to back. Find the claim, then find what is offered as evidence for it, then decide whether you believe the link. Introduction and discussion tell you what the authors think they showed; methods and results tell you what they showed.

## What to keep

Read with a purpose and you have something to write down at the end: the claim, the evidence, the one design decision worth stealing, and the objection you'd raise if you were reviewing it. If nothing goes in the notes, the pass was the wrong pass.
