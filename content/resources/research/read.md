# Read

[How to Read a Paper](http://svr-sk818-web.cl.cam.ac.uk/keshav/papers/07/paper-reading.pdf) by S. Keshav (ACM SIGCOMM CCR, 2007).

[How to Read a Technical Paper](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~jason/advice/how-to-read-a-paper.html) by Jason Eisner (2009)

***

Those two answer different questions, and a reading session needs both. Keshav decides how far to read. Eisner governs what happens during the read.

_**Keshav: three passes, each ending in a decision.**_ The first is five to ten minutes and produces the five Cs: category, context, correctness, contributions, clarity. It ends by deciding whether to continue, and three reasons to stop are legitimate: the paper does not interest you, you do not know enough to understand it, or the assumptions are invalid. The second pass reads for content but skips proofs, takes up to an hour, and its test is whether you can summarize the main thrust with its supporting evidence to someone else. The third pass attempts to virtually re-implement the paper, making the same assumptions and re-creating the work, which is what surfaces hidden assumptions rather than only innovations.

_**Keshav's literature survey, which does not start from a query.**_ Find five recent highly cited papers, do one pass each, read their related work sections. Then find shared citations and repeated author names, which identify the key papers and researchers, and follow those researchers to the venues they publish in. Then scan recent proceedings of those venues. Two passes through the result, iterating on any paper they all cite that you missed.

_**Eisner: posture.**_ Write as you read, at two levels, annotations on specifics and a summary for later retrieval. Read creatively, asking what you would do next. When stuck, switch to background reading rather than pushing harder at the same dense text.

_**What the measurement literature adds.**_ Abstracts decide inclusion badly. Across 139,467 citations and 329,332 screening decisions by 86 reviewers, the abstract screening error rate was 10.76%; 18.07% passed the abstract stage and 5.48% survived to final inclusion. Roughly two thirds of what an abstract admits is overturned once the body is read. Structured extraction runs around 92% accurate until the information is not in the paper, at which point reproducibility falls to 77.2% and the failures take the form of plausible invented content. So _not stated_ has to be an available answer.
