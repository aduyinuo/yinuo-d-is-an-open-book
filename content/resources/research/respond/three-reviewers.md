# Three Reviewers

## Who actually reads the response

The response is addressed to three reviewers, but it is read inside a discussion none of the authors can enter. Area chairs, or program committee members in that role, encourage reviewers to discuss a submission with each other and with the chair, in notes visible only to them, and they attempt to reconcile disagreements between reviews.

Three consequences follow, and together they are the reason a multi-reviewer response is not three single-reviewer responses stapled together.

**Everything is read by everyone.** A concession made to one reviewer is read by the other two. So is a refusal. A response that tells one reviewer the system is more capable than it looks, and separately tells another that the evaluation is thin, has to survive both being read side by side.

**The chair is synthesizing, not adjudicating line by line.** What reaches the decision is a summary of whether the objections were met. Structure a chair can skim beats prose that rewards close reading.

**The authors are absent from the conversation that decides.** Whatever the response does not settle gets settled without them. That argues for closing questions rather than opening them, and for not leaving a disagreement between reviewers for the panel to resolve unaided.

## Write for the likely advocate

The security community states the purpose of a response directly: solidify the champion's support, and give that reviewer what they need to defend the work. The same advice appears elsewhere as arming the champion, and as emboldening a possible advocate who will argue on the authors' behalf.

The reason is structural rather than tactical. The decision happens in a room the authors are not in, so the only instrument they have there is a reviewer who already likes the paper and now has the facts. Every section can be tested against one question: does this give the likely advocate something usable?

## Shared concerns are worth naming as shared

When a criticism is raised by more than one reviewer, saying so in the response is standard practice and does real work. It signals that the reviews were read together rather than triaged separately, and it lets one answer discharge two obligations without appearing to skip one.

It also changes the arithmetic of the word budget. If four comments across three reviews say the evaluation is narrow, answering them once, and saying that is what is happening, costs a fraction of answering them four times.

## Disagreements between reviewers

The harder case, and the one with the most upside.

When two reviewers read the same thing differently, the response can use the disagreement rather than tiptoe around it. Cataloging the points from every review systematically is what surfaces these. Indirect contradictions between reviewers are easy to miss when reviews are read one at a time.

Two moves are available.

**Cite the other reviewer.** Where one reviewer infers that an environment must be simplistic and another credits the same testbed as realistic, the second assessment is evidence, and it costs about nine words to invoke. It is also more persuasive than the authors asserting the same thing, because it comes from inside the panel. The restriction is that this works for a technical point the others may have missed, and never for praise. Committee members read a cherry-picked endorsement as playing reviewers against each other.

**Do not adjudicate between them.** Saying one reviewer is right and another wrong asks the panel to take sides in a dispute the authors started. Supply the fact that resolves it and let them draw the conclusion.

## Protecting the ground you already hold

A response can lose ground it already had. Whatever the reviews credited is an asset, and no concession may spend it.

The trap is a concession that goes further than the objection required. If one reviewer doubts the realism of a testbed and another named that same testbed as a strength, conceding the doubt discards the strongest positive statement in the reviews. Read what the comment actually requires before agreeing to it. The narrower concession, about how a quantity was reported or where a boundary was stated, usually costs nothing and answers the comment in full.

The other half of the same rule: concede cleanly or not at all. A half-concession that hedges reads as evasion, which the ICLR data lists among the negatives.

## Ordering

Nothing in the venue guidance requires answering reviewers in order, and there are better principles.

**Weight by what can move.** Initial score position bounds how far a review can shift, so words spent on a reviewer who is far from the line buy less than words spent on one near it.

**Lead with what is answerable now.** A correction or a completed baseline lands harder than a promise, and it sets the tone for how the rest is read.

**Group by concern, not by reviewer.** A paragraph per theme, naming which reviewers raised it, reads as a synthesis. A paragraph per reviewer reads as three separate conversations that happen to share a page.

## Two things that never go in the response

**Bias.** Both Noble and Sap route a fairness concern to the editor or area chair, privately and separately. In the response it reads as a complaint and it spends credibility. Venue guidelines are blunt about the stronger version: criticizing the reviews or the reviewers is the one thing that can end a submission outright.

**Visible strategy.** Wording that treats the panel as an audience to be played is an etiquette failure even when the reasoning under it is sound.
