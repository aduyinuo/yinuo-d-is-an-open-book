# Drafting

The seventh step of the [procedure](procedure.md) is the only one that produces response text. It writes one section per cluster of comments.

## The shape of one answer

Four sentence roles, in order.

<table><thead><tr><th width="150">Role</th><th>What it does</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Answer</strong></td><td>The direct answer to the question as asked, first, before any background.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Ground</strong></td><td>The fact, number, equation, or passage that supports it.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Consequence</strong></td><td>What follows, in both registers: what it means formally, and what it means for whoever would use the system.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Change</strong></td><td>What the revision will do, stated so a shepherd can check completion.</td></tr>
</tbody></table>

Answer first, explain second, is Rule 7 of Noble's ten. A reviewer scanning for whether you addressed their point should not have to read a paragraph to find out.

An explanation that stops at the mechanism leaves the reviewer to compute the implication, and the implication is the part they asked about. A number appears only where it changes a decision, since a number with no denominator and no use is read as evidence and displaces evidence that would have done work. Depth and clarity are one requirement rather than a trade: the venues limit scope and say nothing about depth, so a shallow answer recovers nothing.

A conceded limitation gets its own paragraph rather than being folded into a defense, and the paragraph needs three parts. The concern, stated plainly in the field's own terms. The limitation, bounded, with the reason it is bounded. What would settle the question. Where the reviewer's phrasing of the limitation is inaccurate, refute the phrasing and honor the concern, in that order, in the same passage.

## Titles and the first clause

**A title names the kind of engagement before the subject.** A clarification of, a consideration of, a misunderstanding about, the hidden assumptions of. A title that names only a topic tells the reader nothing about what the section does with it. Sap's version of the same rule: prefer a heading that states the answer over one that restates the complaint, since a heading naming the weakness makes the reviewer reread their own objection.

**The first clause names the concern in the reviewer's own vocabulary.** A correction the reader cannot attach to a question is a true sentence answering nothing, and in a document read by three people with different concerns, nobody does the work of matching it. Echoing a distinctive phrase from the review costs about ten words per paragraph. It is the last thing to cut, not the first.

The corollary is a deletion rule. A sentence with no reviewer comment behind it is removed.

## The acknowledgment is written in the functional voice

This is the failure that recurs through the most drafts. Describing the reviewer is the wrong way to write an acknowledgment. A sentence that narrates what a reviewer did, in front of them and to an audience of their colleagues, reads as an accusation even when it is accurate, and it spends its words on the accusation rather than on the answer.

Speaking to them is the right way, and the vocabulary is functional. _For clarification_ introduces a fact they lacked. _We agree that_ concedes. _Regarding_ opens a topic without assigning blame. _On whether_ takes up a question as a question. None of these say who was wrong, and none of them need to. Security practice uses the same device with a smaller vocabulary, as a leading marker word: Correction, Clarification, Change, Offer.

## Postures

**When the reviewer misread.** Correct graciously, written so that the misreading was a reasonable one. The form is an observation that there appears to be a misunderstanding, followed by the correct fact, and never a statement that the reviewer is wrong. Rule 3 of Noble's ten is to treat the misreading as evidence that the text was unclear, and this is not only diplomacy. A reviewer who misread a passage is a sample of one from the population of future readers, so the misreading is data about the paper. Where the paper already states the point, saying it will be clarified in a revised version does the same work without correcting anyone.

**When the reviewer found a defect.** Agree first. Thank them where they spelled out the fix, because that is labor done for you. Then the fix. If the error was yours, supply the fixed fact and do not dwell on how it arose. Announcing culpability is not the same as fixing the thing, and every extra word of apology holds the reader's attention on the fault.

**When you decline.** Both halves in one paragraph, always: refute the phrasing where it is inaccurate, and honor the concern underneath it. A refusal without the second half reads as defensive. A concession without the first half gives up ground the reviewer did not ask for.

**When you offer.** Cooperative, without appearing eager. Several options, each with what it would show. Promise with _will_, never _might_ or _could consider_. Never volunteer ongoing work, since a mentioned work in progress converts into a revision requirement.

**Do what was asked when you can.** Rule 8: even an experiment you consider uninformative is usually cheaper to run than to argue against, and running it removes the objection rather than contesting it.

**Write it twice.** Rule 10: draft once to work out what you think and to get the irritation out, then write the version that gets sent. The two documents have different jobs and should not be the same file.

## A phrasing shelf

* Thank once, at the top, for the labor invested in the reviews. Editors read per-comment compliments as obsequious, and they cost words. The exception is a comment that arrives with the fix spelled out, which is guidance rather than criticism and is thanked where it is answered.
* Third person, not second: _we agree with the reviewer_, not _we agree with you_. The response is read by the whole panel and by the chairs, not only by its addressee.
* Say _we agree_, not _we acknowledge_. The second sounds like fault where none is conceded.
* Say _a revised version_, not _the final version_. The second presumes acceptance.
* Grade a direct answer to the size of the error: yes, no, not quite, almost.
* Do not validate. _That is a fair point_, _the concern is valid_, _the reviewer is right that_: these grant permission the reviewer did not ask for and quietly reverse who is judging whom. Acknowledge with the fact instead.

## What gets cut first

When a response runs long, the cuts come from the same three places, and none of them costs anything.

**Restatement of the manuscript.** The reviewers have read the paper. Repeating it spends the budget twice on the same content and carries a faint suggestion that they missed something. What counts as new: a number the paper never states, a correction, a commitment, a fact the text got wrong, an inference the reviewer did not draw. The one exception is where the paper's own statement is the thing being corrected, since the reader needs the corrected version in front of them.

**Spectator voice.** The response is written by the people who did the work, and they state their own numbers rather than reconstructing them from published figures in front of the reader. The tells are _from the figures already in it_, _reading the reported numbers_, _if we compute_, _working through the arithmetic_. Give the result. If the derivation matters it belongs in the revised paper.

**Pointing instead of answering.** A section number, a file, or an appendix is not an answer. The reviewer has already read the section, which is how they formed the comment, and pointing at it is a validated negative.
