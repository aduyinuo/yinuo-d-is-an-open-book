# Think

## Finding a problem

The gap between an interesting topic and a question you can answer this year is where most projects die. A researchable problem has three properties: you can say what would count as an answer, you can say what would count as being wrong, and the answer would change what someone does.

"Finding researchable problems" is worth reading precisely because it treats problem-finding as a skill with steps rather than a matter of taste — narrow the topic until the question is answerable, then check that it is still worth answering.

## How to think about it once you have it

**[How to Solve It](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691164076/how-to-solve-it) — George Pólya.**

Four stages: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, look back. The heuristics in the middle are the famous part — solve a simpler version, work backwards from the goal, find a related problem you already solved, vary the problem until it becomes tractable, ask what the unknown actually is.

The stage everyone skips is the fourth. Looking back — can you check the result differently, can you get it more directly, can the method be reused — is the only one that compounds across problems.

## What expertise changes

**[There's more to mathematics than rigour and proofs](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/theres-more-to-mathematics-than-rigour-and-proofs/) — Terence Tao.**

Three stages of development. *Pre-rigorous*: intuition, hand-waving, examples, no formal apparatus. *Rigorous*: the formalism, applied carefully and somewhat pedantically, intuition deliberately suspended. *Post-rigorous*: the formalism internalized to the point where intuition can be trusted again — because you now know exactly which intuitions the formalism licenses.

The trap is thinking the second stage is the destination. Rigour is the tool that makes intuition reliable; the point was never to stop having intuitions.

**[Intuition and logic in mathematics](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Poincare_Intuition/) — Henri Poincaré.** A century old, and still the clearest statement that discovery and verification are different acts, done well by different habits of mind, and that a field which honours only one of them will produce people who can check work but not start it.
