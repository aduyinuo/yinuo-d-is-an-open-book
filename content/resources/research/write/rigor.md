# Rigor

**[The Craft of Research](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo23576265.html) — Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup & Fitzgerald.**

Not a style guide, and not a methods textbook. It is about what an argument is made of, and it is the book I'd hand to anyone who can produce results but can't yet turn them into a paper someone should believe.

The core apparatus is five moving parts. A **claim** — what you want the reader to accept. **Reasons** — why they should. **Evidence** — what the reasons rest on, which must be something the reader can check rather than something they have to take from you. **Acknowledgment and response** — the objections a competent sceptic would raise, stated in their strongest form and then answered. And **warrants** — the general principle connecting your evidence to your claim, which is usually left implicit and is usually where the argument actually breaks.

Two things it changed for me.

**The reader is not a receptacle.** Writing is a transaction with someone who has their own reasons to doubt you, and the acknowledgment-and-response move is the whole difference between a paper that persuades and one that merely reports. Skipping it doesn't make the objection go away; it makes the reviewer raise it instead.

**The question comes before the topic.** The book's formula — I am studying X, because I want to find out Y, in order to help my reader understand Z — is a good filter. If the "in order to" is empty, the project is a topic, not a question.

It's also the best short treatment I know of research ethics as a practical matter rather than a compliance one: the obligation to report what didn't work, not to overclaim, and to make the reader's job of checking you possible.
