# Mathematical Writing

## Notation hygiene

**[How to Write Mathematics](https://www.math.lsu.edu/~oxley/halmos.pdf) — Paul Halmos** (1970).

Say something, say it in order, say it plainly. Halmos's specific advice is what makes it useful: think about the reader's memory budget before introducing a symbol; never use two symbols for one thing or one symbol for two things; the spiral plan — introduce a special case, then generalize, rather than stating the general result cold; and write in complete sentences even when the sentence contains an equation.

**[Mathematical Writing](https://www-cs-faculty.stanford.edu/~knuth/klr.html) — Knuth, Larrabee & Roberts.**

Notes from Knuth's 1987 Stanford class, with guest lectures from Lamport, Halmos, Ullman, van Leunen and others. The first handout is a list of twenty-six specific rules, most of which are about the seam between prose and symbols: don't start a sentence with a symbol; don't put two formulas in a row separated only by a comma; define notation before using it and use it consistently after; keep displayed equations punctuated as part of the sentence they belong to.

**Good mathematical writing.** The short version, for when you don't have time for either of the above: every symbol earns its keep, every equation is part of a sentence, and any notation you have to look back for is notation you should have avoided.

## The pitch

**[On Proof and Progress in Mathematics](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9404236) — William Thurston.**

Written as a response to a debate about rigour, and the deepest thing on this page. Thurston's claim: mathematicians advance human understanding of mathematics, and the formal proof is the residue of that understanding rather than the substance of it. He describes the many mental models people hold for the same object — the derivative as slope, as rate, as limit, as approximation, as symbol — and points out that communication happens at the level of models, not of proofs. Which is why a technically complete paper can transmit almost nothing.

**[There's more to mathematics than rigour and proofs](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/theres-more-to-mathematics-than-rigour-and-proofs/) — Terence Tao.** Also filed under [Think](../think.md); it belongs in both places, because the post-rigorous stage is what makes a paper readable.

**Writing the paper — William Thomson.** Practical, economics-flavoured, and unsentimental about the fact that a paper is a thing you are asking a busy stranger to read.
