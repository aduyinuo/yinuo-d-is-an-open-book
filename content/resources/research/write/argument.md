# Argument

[**The Craft of Research**](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo23576265.html) **, 4th edition** (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup & FitzGerald, University of Chicago Press, 2016), Part III, _Making an Argument_, from p. 105.

[Rigor](rigor.md) is the short account of what this book is for. This page is the working version of Part III, because the five elements do a second job the book does not describe: they sort reviewer comments. A reviewer's objection is always an objection to one of the five, and knowing which one determines what an answer can consist of.

## The five elements

The authors derive the elements from the questions a skeptical listener asks (7.1, p. 111):

1. **Claim.** What do you want me to believe? What is your point?
2. **Reasons.** Why do you say that? Why should I agree?
3. **Evidence.** How do you know? Can you back it up?
4. **Acknowledgment and response.** But what about the alternatives?
5. **Warrant.** How does that follow? Can you explain your reasoning?

The core is one line: claim, because of reason, based on evidence (7.2.2, p. 114). A claim is an assertion that demands support. A reason is an assertion that supports a claim. Evidence is data deployed to support a reason, and unlike the other two it is not always framed as an assertion (p. 112).

Two properties of a claim are assessed separately from its support. It must be specific, and it must be significant. Significance is defined operationally: it is measured by how much the claim asks readers to change what they think, estimated as the number of other beliefs they would have to revise on accepting it (8.2.2, p. 127).

## Reasons are not evidence

This is the distinction writers most often collapse, and collapsing it produces arguments that look supported and are not.

The test is a pair of sentences (7.2.2, p. 113). "What evidence do you base your reason on?" is well formed. "What reason do you base your evidence on?" is not. Two differences follow. Reasons are thought up. Evidence has to be searched for in the world and then made available for everyone to see. Hence the diagnostic: it makes no sense to ask where to go and see your reasons, and it makes perfect sense to ask where to go and see your evidence.

The reason writers conflate the two is that a chain of reasons feels like support. A reason is also a subclaim, and a subclaim can take reasons of its own, so the chain can run a long way without touching data. Eventually you have to show some.

Where the chain stops is not the writer's decision. Chapter 9 makes it a property of the audience: to count as evidence, a statement must report something readers agree not to question, at least for the purposes of the argument (9.2, p. 133). If they do question it, what you took for hard evidence is only a soft reason, and the argument has not reached bottom.

Chapter 9 adds a complication that matters for empirical work. A paper almost never contains the evidence. It contains a report of the evidence: a table, a figure, a summary statistic. Data taken from a source were already shaped by that source, and reporting them again shapes them once more, if only by placing them in a new context. State how the data were collected and by whom, and cite a source as close to the evidence as you can get.

The criteria are a list of five (9.4, p. 138). Readers want evidence to be accurate, precise, sufficient, representative, and authoritative. Relevance is treated separately, because relevance is a question about the warrant.

## Warrants

A warrant is a principle that connects a reason to a claim (chapter 11, p. 155). It exists because a reader can grant that a reason is true and still deny that it is relevant.

A warrant has two parts, a general circumstance and a general consequence, and its logic is that if a generalization holds, so must specific instances of it. For the inference to go through, a reader has to accept four things: that the warrant is reasonable, that the reason is true, that the circumstance in the reason is a plausible instance of the general circumstance, and that the consequence in the claim is a plausible instance of the general consequence (7.4, p. 116). Most academic warrants are stated in compressed form, with the two parts run together, but both can always be recovered.

**When to state one.** State a warrant only if readers will not understand the reasoning without it, or if you expect the reasoning to be challenged (chapter 11, p. 155). Section 11.5 (p. 164) names three occasions: the readers are outside the field, the principle of reasoning is new or controversial within the field, or the claim is one readers will resist because they do not want it to be true. In the third case the recommended order puts a warrant they already accept before the reason and the claim.

The converse is equally explicit. Experienced researchers writing for specialists leave most warrants unstated, because stating the obvious reads as condescension rather than help (11.2, p. 158). That is why arguments written for specialists are hard for newcomers to follow. The connecting principle is present and invisible.

**Five tests** (11.4, p. 161): is the warrant reasonable, is it sufficiently limited, is it superior to competing warrants, is it appropriate to this field, and is it able to cover the reason and the claim. The last test is the one that catches bad arguments that look valid. The book works an example in which the warrant is acceptable, the reason is true, and the argument still fails, because the stated reason is not an instance of the warrant's general circumstance (11.6, p. 166).

**Prefer evidence.** A claim of fact cannot be supported by a warrant and a reason alone. Where data are available, rely on hard evidence rather than on elaborate lines of reasoning built from warrants (Quick Tip, p. 172).

## Acknowledgment and response

This is the element that separates an argument from an assertion. Chapter 10 states it as a condition:

> An argument is not complete if it fails to recognize other points of view.
>
> Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup & FitzGerald, _The Craft of Research_, 4th ed., p. 141

A text offering only claims, reasons, and evidence may still read as thin, or as ignorant of the views it did not name.

Anticipated objections sort into two kinds (p. 142). Objections to **intrinsic** soundness challenge the clarity of the claim, the relevance of the reasons, or the quality of the evidence. Objections to **extrinsic** soundness ask you to consider alternatives: a different framing of the problem, evidence you overlooked, or what others have written. The second kind is usually found by reading sources for disagreement rather than for data, including disagreements among the sources themselves (10.2, p. 145).

Section 10.1 (pp. 143 to 144) lists the questions a hostile reader asks of the support. One asks for a different kind of evidence. The rest attack quality: not accurate, not precise enough, not current, not representative, not authoritative. The hardest to answer is the one that asks for more of it.

A response is not a competing claim. Even a minimal response supplies a reason for limiting or rejecting what was acknowledged, and a substantial one is a subordinate argument with its own reasons, evidence, and possibly its own warrant (10.4, p. 148).

Two points carry directly into an author response. First, acknowledging a flaw you cannot fix is recommended over concealing it, with three usable forms: that the rest of the argument outweighs the flaw, that the flaw is serious but further work will find a way around it, or that the flaw prevents full acceptance while the argument still shows what a better answer would need (10.3.2, p. 147). Second, on phrasing disagreement: save criticism for the response, and direct it at the work rather than at the person (10.5.1, p. 150).

Selection is a real constraint. Section 10.3 (p. 145) calls it a Goldilocks problem. Too many acknowledgments distract from the core, too few make you seem indifferent to the reader's views. The priority order: plausible charges you can rebut, alternative lines of argument important in the field, alternative conclusions readers want to be true, alternative evidence readers already know, and counterexamples that have to be addressed.

## Testing a draft by asking a reader's questions

Run the five questions against the draft before a reader does. Write the core first, then question it as someone with a stake in a different outcome would. The posture the book suggests is not warfare but a warm debate, adopted on behalf of a reader who wants the argument to be wrong. The cost logic attached to it: the time to fix a problem with an argument is when you find it.

The Quick Tip after chapter 10 (pp. 153 to 154) adds three disagreements that some readers always produce. There are causes in addition to the one claimed. There are counterexamples. And, third, I do not define X as you do. On the last, definitions function as warrants, so a dispute over a definition is a dispute over the connection between a reason and a claim, not a matter of vocabulary.

## Reading a reviewer comment as an attack on one element

A review is the reader's half of the conversation, delivered after the fact. Every substantive objection lands on one of five places. Identifying which one is faster than parsing the comment sentence by sentence, and it determines what the answer can consist of.

Three consequences follow.

**An objection to the warrant cannot be answered with more evidence.** The reviewer has already granted the reason. Adding a table addresses a question nobody asked. The answer has to state the principle that makes the reason relevant, then show that the reason and the claim are good instances of it. If neither can be shown, the argument needs revision rather than defense (11.6, p. 168).

**An objection to the evidence cannot be answered with more reasoning.** The same rule read in the other direction. A claim of fact does not follow from a warrant and a reason. If a reviewer says the evaluation is thin, the answer is a run, a number, or a stated limit.

**An objection to a definition is an objection to a warrant.** Reviewers frequently write that a word in the paper does not mean what the paper takes it to mean. Treating that as a terminology complaint understates it. The definition licenses the inference, so the answer owes a definition and a short argument for choosing it, including an acknowledgment of the competing sense and a reason for not using it.

One more use of the frame. When the same objection arrives from two reviewers in different words, sorting both by target shows whether it is one objection or two. Two comments that both target the evidence can be answered once. A comment on the evidence and a comment on the warrant cannot, however similar their wording. See [Respond](../respond/) for what to do with the sorted list.

## A note on provenance

The five elements, the definitions, and every page reference above are the book's. The table and the three consequences in the previous section are not. They apply the book's frame to reviewer comments, which is an extension the authors do not make.
