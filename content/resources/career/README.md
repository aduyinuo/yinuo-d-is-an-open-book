# Career

Advice about academic careers ages badly when it's about tactics and well when it's about disposition. These are the ones that held up.

## Terence Tao, [Career advice](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/)

A collection arranged by career stage, unusually free of mystique. Written for mathematicians; almost none of it is field-specific.

The entries I return to: *talk to your advisor* — they are the single highest-bandwidth source you have, and most students underuse them out of embarrassment. *Make your work available* — put the CV and publication list somewhere public and keep it current. *Learn the limitations of your tools*, and *ask yourself dumb questions*, which is the same advice twice: the questions you're embarrassed to ask are the ones where your model is actually broken. *Enjoy your work* — not as sentiment, but because the effort required is not sustainable otherwise.

## Gian-Carlo Rota, [Ten Lessons I Wish I Had Been Taught](https://www.ams.org/notices/199701/comm-rota.pdf)

Four pages, Notices of the AMS, 1997. Cheerfully unsentimental.

Every lecture should make only one main point, and you should say it at least three times — beginning, middle, end. Never run over time. Publish the same result several times, in different framings, because nobody read it the first time. Write informative introductions: most people read only the introduction, and if it's a summary of the sections they learn nothing. Be prepared for old age, and note that mathematicians are judged on a small number of results, not on the pile.

The uncomfortable one: you are more likely to be remembered for an expository paper than for a technical result.

## William Thomson, advice for graduate students

Written for economists. The parts that transfer: how to choose a problem that can actually be finished, what a supervisor is and is not for, and how to tell the difference between a project that is hard and a project that is stuck.
