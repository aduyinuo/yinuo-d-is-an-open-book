# Problem-Solving in Small Groups

<figure><img src="../../../.gitbook/assets/ps-winter-survival.gif" alt="Winter survival task: individual rankings converging on a group ranking, against the expert ranking"><figcaption>Four people rank twelve items. The group then has to agree on one ranking.</figcaption></figure>

## The task

The winter survival task. A plane goes down in a frozen forest and twelve salvaged items have to be ranked by how much they matter to staying alive — a lighter, newspaper, a compass, gauze, a knife, an air map, canvas, a shirt, whisky, chocolate, rope, an ax.

There is an expert ranking, so error is measurable. Each person ranks alone first. Then the group has to produce one ranking together.

## What the animation shows

Faint dots are the individual rankings, spread out. The dark dot is the group's consensus as it forms. The short dashes are the expert answer.

The number at the bottom is the point. Mean error for individuals, mean error for the group. The group usually beats the average member — pooling helps — but it rarely beats its *best* member, and that gap is where the interesting failure lives. A group that could recognise who among them already knows would outperform the group that averages.

## Why it's a good instrument

Ranking tasks make disagreement legible. You can see exactly which items the group fought over, who moved, and whether the movement was toward the better answer or just toward the loudest person. That makes it a workable testbed for consensus processes — including ones where some of the participants are language models rather than people.
