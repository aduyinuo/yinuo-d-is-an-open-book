# Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma

## Dynamic Sociality

<figure><img src="../../../../.gitbook/assets/social-ipd-network.gif" alt="Agents playing iterated prisoner&#x27;s dilemma on a network"><figcaption><p>Eight agents, twelve ties, fourteen rounds. Green edges are mutual cooperation.</p></figcaption></figure>

Each agent plays its neighbours repeatedly, and each carries a fixed strategy — tit-for-tat, always cooperate, always defect, grim. Nobody sees the strategy list; they only see what happened last round.

Watch what the always-defect nodes do to their neighbourhoods. A tit-for-tat agent next to a defector stops cooperating, and its other ties go cold too. The damage is not confined to the pair that caused it, because the retaliating agent cannot separate the neighbour who exploited it from the neighbours who didn't. Payoffs on the right make the cost visible: defection pays early and then starves the neighbourhood it depends on.

## Catagorization

<figure><img src="../../../../.gitbook/assets/social-categorization.gif" alt="One agent categorising nine partners into types as evidence accumulates"><figcaption><p>One agent, nine partners, sorted and re-sorted as evidence arrives.</p></figcaption></figure>

The second animation drops the network and takes a single agent's point of view. It plays each partner in turn and keeps a running estimate of how often each one cooperates.

It does not track nine separate people. It compresses them into a small number of types — cooperative, conditional, exploitative — because carrying nine independent models is not what people do.

The categories are relative, not absolute. A partner is classified against the group mean, not against a fixed threshold, so the same behaviour lands in different categories depending on who else is in the room. Move a mildly cooperative partner into a generous group and they become the exploiter; move them into a harsh one and they become the cooperator. That is the contrast effect, and it is doing real work here: identical evidence, different judgement.

## _Contrast_

Contrast is the dependence of a judgement about one partner on the distribution of the others. Identical evidence, different judgement, because the comparison set moved. It is not a threshold that drifts with experience, which is a property of one relationship over time. This is a property of the population at one moment, and it changes the judgement of a partner who has done nothing different. Hold one partner's behaviour fixed and replace the other partners. If the assigned category changes, the mechanism is contrast. If it does not, the classifier is absolute however it was described.

In the model it is not the category assignment step. It is the retrieval step. Activation is augmented by a stereotype score, the similarity of the observed action to the assigned category's typical behaviour, multiplied by a category-level term that grows with how far that category sits from the other active ones. Distinctive partners are attended to more.

The field already has an account of exactly this. Parducci's range-frequency theory says a judgement is a compromise between a range principle, which spreads the categories over the observed range, and a frequency principle, which gives each category the same number of stimuli, so a judgement of one item depends on the distribution of the others. That is the citation this section needs, because it says the relative classification is a documented property of human judgement rather than a modelling convenience.

_**The printed formula runs backwards.**_ The category-level term is printed as a weighted cosine similarity between category prototype vectors. Read literally, larger contrast should correspond to more dissimilar categories and not to more similar ones, and the expression is undefined at a zero-norm prototype. The surviving code computes a weighted distance between the same vectors, which is what the mechanism's stated purpose needs. The distance form, bounded into $$[0,1)$$, is the specification I adopt. The printed similarity form is kept in the codebase as a named variant and run only as a check.

_**And the assignment is relative in one branch only.**_ The paragraph above says a partner is classified against the group mean rather than against a fixed threshold. The implementation matches first against fixed named prototypes by distance, and falls back to clustering the currently active fingerprints only when no confident match is found. So the assignment step is absolute where a prototype fits and relative where none does, while the contrast term at retrieval is always relative. Either the prototype branch goes, or that sentence acquires a clause.

_**The result is a null, and it is a null of the mechanism rather than of the code.**_ Contrast, reconstructed to match its own stated psychological purpose and ablated against its own reference conditions, does not produce the predicted discrimination benefit in any ecology or statistical design tried, and shows a small, consistent payoff cost instead. An earlier draft gated the term on a condition that could never hold, so it did nothing by construction; the current one measurably changes the prosociality trajectories, and a regression test holds it to that. The mechanism is on this page because it is the psychological claim the theory makes. The ablation is in the report because the claim did not survive contact with its own reference conditions.

Both of those printed-against-implemented problems were found by reimplementing the model rather than by rereading it. That is an argument for reimplementation as a reviewing method.

## What the network result actually turns on

The first animation shows one defector cooling a neighbourhood. The manipulated version of that setting is three topologies at constant group size, fifteen agents over one hundred rounds, and two levels of one ratio.

_**Degree of interdependence.**_ $$k$$ is the proportion of outcome variance attributable to the partner's behaviour, $$k = PC / (AC + PC)$$ over the three components of interdependence theory: actor control, how much your own action determines your outcome, partner control, how much theirs does, and joint control, how much the combination does. At $$k = 0$$ outcomes are entirely self-determined; at $$k = 1$$ they depend entirely on the partner. It is a ratio and not a payoff difference, and it is what decides whether structure matters at all. High interdependence, $$k = 1.00$$, pays five for matching and zero otherwise. Low interdependence, $$k = 0.09$$, makes one action nearly dominant, and there all three topologies converge and the structure is irrelevant.

_**Topology.**_ Disconnected dyads, one fixed partner each, seven edges. A lattice, a ring with a few cross-links, seventeen to nineteen. Fully connected, one hundred and five, with the partner drawn at random each round. Group size is held constant, so what varies is not how many agents there are but how the encounters are distributed among them. Can an agent predict who it will play next? Certainly in a dyad, approximately in a lattice, not at all fully connected. That is the ordering that matters, not the edge count.

_**The connectivity paradox.**_ Under high interdependence, sparse networks outperform dense ones. Maximisation reaches $$.84$$ in disconnected dyads, $$.63$$ in the lattice and $$.51$$ fully connected, and the human experiment reproduces the ordering. This contradicts the information-diffusion result, where dense networks converge faster on a shared solution, and the difference is the task: diffusion spreads an answer, coordination requires the other party to move at the same time. It is not an aggregation artifact, and that was checked by re-indexing per trial rather than per round, which holds observation count constant and leaves the dyadic advantage intact.

_**Encounter frequency is what reinterprets the whole finding.**_ In a disconnected dyad each pair meets on every round, accumulating one hundred encounters over one hundred rounds. Fully connected with fifteen agents, random matching yields roughly seven encounters per pair over the same horizon. Re-index coordination by per-pair encounter count and the three curves collapse onto one. Agents in all three structures learn at the same rate per encounter, so the sparse advantage is about how many encounters fit in the window and not about any structural benefit to learning. Experience is per agent and accumulates faster in dense networks; per-pair encounters run the other way, and the second is what predicts coordination.

_**Win-stay lose-shift.**_ Repeat the last action after a rewarding outcome, switch after a poor one. It is the mechanism behind the dyadic advantage and it needs no representation of the partner at all, which is what separates it from tit-for-tat: tit-for-tat conditions on the partner's last action, win-stay lose-shift needs only your own payoff. It fails in dense networks not because agents abandon it but because the structure makes it incoherent, since the partner required to complete the coordination is not guaranteed to be present. Sustained at $$.778$$ under individual memory in every topology and lower in every topology under pooled memory, with the widest gap fully connected. In the human experiment its use differs by condition, $$F(2,26) = 4.20$$, $$p = .026$$.

## Inside the agent

_**Instance.**_ One stored experience. Under minimal information it is a pair, my action and my outcome. Under identity it is five slots: partner index, my action, the partner's action, my outcome, the partner's outcome. It is not a state-action value. An instance is a specific past episode retrieved by similarity rather than a running average, and two agents with identical averages can behave differently because their instances differ in recency. The move from two slots to five is the whole difference between the minimal-information model and the identity model.

_**Blended value.**_ The value of an action, computed as a retrieval-probability weighted average over the instances consistent with it, with the partner's outcome entering weighted by the prosociality value. Not an expected value under a learned distribution. Blending is over what was retrieved, so it inherits recency and frequency effects and the noise term, and two agents with the same history can choose differently. Decay $$0.5$$, mismatch penalty $$1$$, noise $$0.25$$, the standard defaults, held across every condition so that differences are attributable to the mechanisms and not to the fit.

_**Individual against pooled memory.**_ Individual memory retrieves only instances recorded against the partner currently faced. Pooled memory retrieves from every partner, treating all past interactions as equally relevant. The middle term is category-conditional retrieval, which retrieves from partners sharing a category, and pooled is that middle term with one category containing everyone. Does the retrieval filter carry a partner index? None is pooled, an exact index is individual, a category label is the middle. Pooled memory creates a strategic averaging problem: instead of learning cooperate with cooperators and defect against defectors, agents learn averaged responses that perform moderately against all opponent types and excel against none. The gap is widest fully connected and narrowest in the ring.

_**So compression is a constraint, not a strategy that pays.**_ The section above argues compression from a claim about people, that carrying nine independent models is not what people do. The simulations find the opposite ordering on performance: individual, fully separated memory beats pooled memory on every metric in every topology. And granted perfect individuation, the bottleneck in dense networks is not memory capacity but encounter scarcity. Three results, one direction each, and they are compatible only if compression is read as a constraint people are under. That reading has a consequence worth stating: categorization should be evaluated as a bounded-capacity account and not as a performance improvement, and any experiment that scores it on payoff alone is scoring it against a claim nobody made. The one family of conditions where category learning does pay on cumulative outcome as well as on discrimination is bounded memory, a cap on retained instances per agent, which is exactly what a compression account predicts.

_**Dynamic prosociality.**_ A per-partner weight on the partner's outcome inside the blended value, updated after every interaction from the signed gap between what was expected and what arrived. When it is high, an agent weighs the partner's payoff alongside its own; when it is low, the agent acts largely in self-interest. It is not a social value orientation, which is a trait of the agent and one number. This is a property of a relationship, so one agent carries as many values as it has partners and they diverge. Does the quantity have a partner index? A single social preference for the whole population is an orientation; one per tie is prosociality in this sense.

The corrected update is a two-branch rule on the sign of the surprise, with the positive branch floored at the current value and the negative branch capped at it, so the value is provably weakly increasing on non-negative surprise and weakly decreasing on negative surprise for any learning rate in $$(0,1]$$. That property is checked by a test rather than assumed from continuity, and it needs checking, because read literally against the printed gap the two branches are swapped. The reconstruction implements the prose. The printed equations need the sign fixed before the paper goes out again.

## Typing a partner

Categorization assigns each partner to a small set of kinds from observed behaviour, and then treats the kind rather than the partner as the unit of memory and of prosociality. It is not partner-indexed memory alone, which keeps every partner separate and shares nothing. Categorization is what lets experience with one partner reach another, and it is the only mechanism here that can act before a partner has been played. Does experience recorded against partner A change the decision about partner B? If not, there is memory but no categorization. It reaches the decision in two places: category-conditional retrieval, and category-level prosociality, where a partner with no update history inherits the mean value of its category on assignment.

A **type** here is a judgement made by one agent about another. It can be wrong, it can change, and it depends on who else is in the room, which is what separates it from the partner's actual policy. Is the label held by an agent, or by the experimenter? An experimenter's label is ground truth; an agent's label is a type in this sense, and the two are scored against each other rather than confused.

What the categoriser actually clusters is a **behavioural fingerprint**: a five-feature summary of one partner's recent behaviour over a sliding window of five rounds, on cooperation rate, reciprocity, forgiveness, retaliation, and volatility. Cooperation rate alone is one of the five. A partner who cooperates half the time at random and a partner who alternates share a cooperation rate and differ on volatility and reciprocity, which is what the other four features are for. Can two partners with the same cooperation rate land in different categories? If not, only one feature is doing work.

A category does not exist until it is **promoted**, which happens when membership and temporal-stability thresholds are met. A prototype match is immediate and needs no history; promotion is the slow route, and its thresholds are what stop a category from being invented out of three rounds of noise. Could the label change next round on one surprising action? Then it is a match, not a promoted category. One consequence is reported rather than patched: zero-shot transfer of a prosociality value is architecturally available only when a category arrives through an external channel before any interaction, an oracle or a reputation signal. For a category learned from the partner's own behaviour it cannot fire, because the confidence requirement guarantees the partner already has its own update history by the time the label exists.

_**Three names, three prototypes, nine categories.**_ The section above names the types by their social reading: cooperative, conditional, exploitative. The model matches a fingerprint against named prototypes that are cooperative, defective and reactive, and clusters only where no confident match is found. And the count is not three: the category cap is nine, matching the appeal to the upper end of Miller's $$7 \pm 2$$ working-memory bound. Two of the three names differ. The page's three is a description of the result and the model's nine is a capacity bound. Conditional and reactive are the same behaviour named for its social meaning and for its mechanism; defective and exploitative are the same partner named from the outside and from the victim's side. The naming has to be settled before either version is taught, because the social names are what a reader will carry away.

## Where two of my files disagree

<table><thead><tr><th width="360">The disagreement</th><th>Where it sits</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Compression as a constraint, or as something that pays</td><td>individual against pooled memory</td></tr><tr><td>The printed prosociality update against its own prose</td><td>dynamic prosociality</td></tr><tr><td>Three social names, three prototype names, nine categories</td><td>type</td></tr><tr><td>Contrast printed as a similarity, implemented as a distance</td><td>contrast</td></tr><tr><td>Classified against the group mean, or matched to fixed prototypes</td><td>contrast</td></tr><tr><td>Type as a compression, and type as a hidden latent</td><td>the entry below</td></tr><tr><td>Dynamic Sociality against Dynamic Prosociality</td><td>the heading of this page</td></tr></tbody></table>

The last two are worth spelling out. In a Bayesian game a type is a private parameter of a player that the others hold a belief over and update by Bayes' rule, and the cluster's [open-problems page](../next.md) uses the word that way when it describes both halves of this branch as one inference, recovering a hidden type from behaviour while the other party adapts. That sentence is the one that unifies the branch and it is worth keeping. But a compression is lossy on purpose and a latent is not, and a category assigned against the group mean is not identified the way a Bayesian type is. If the two halves are folded into one account, that is the first thing the account has to say.

And the heading at the top of this page says Dynamic Sociality while the paper three sections below says Dynamic Prosociality. One concept, two forms, in one file. Prosociality is the one the model carries, where it names a weight on the partner's outcome. The paper title is the form to keep.
