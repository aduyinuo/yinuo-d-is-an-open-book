# Research

I study decision-making in cybersecurity — the machine's, the attacker's, the defender's, and what happens when a person and an agent have to make one decision together.

The hard part of defending a network has never been the network. It is that every consequential move depends on someone else's move: an adversary who adapts, a teammate who may or may not approve, an analyst who has to decide whether the recommendation on the screen is worth trusting. An agent that treats the network as a static environment and the people in it as noise will look excellent in a benchmark and be useless in an operations centre.

So the work runs in four directions, and they are meant to meet.

## Cyber World Modeling

[**Cyber World Modeling**](../overview/cyber-world-modeling/) asks what an agent has to represent in order to act. Not everything: a defender that models the whole state space equally is spending its budget in the wrong place. The two pieces here are a world model that keeps the adversary factored out as its own latent variable, so learning can happen where the network actually is rather than only in a simulator; and a way of asking _where_ in a large game the strategic dependence actually sits, so expensive game-theoretic reasoning can be spent there and nowhere else.

## Mental World Modeling

[**Mental World Modeling**](../overview/mental-world-modeling/) is about the people the agent shares the problem with. How groups solve problems they cannot solve alone — in small teams, in general computing work, in capture-the-flag competitions. How players read each other across repeated interaction and sort each other into kinds. How attackers depart from optimal play in ways that are regular enough to model, because a defender trained against a rational adversary is trained against nobody.

## Human-AI Complementarity

[**Human-AI Complementarity**](../overview/human-ai-complementarity/) takes the position that collaboration is a structure, not an attitude. Who can authorize what, which actions must happen together, what each teammate is allowed to see — write those down explicitly and teamwork becomes something you can vary and measure rather than something you assert afterward. That means building the instruments: testbeds where the team structure itself is the manipulation, and traces detailed enough to say what went wrong rather than only that it did.

## Toward Deployment

[**Toward Deployment**](../overview/toward-deployment/) is the discipline of not believing your own results. What does anyone mean by a "realistic" environment, and can a claim of realism be checked rather than asserted? What does training in a heavier environment cost, and what do you get back? Does a policy learned in one environment survive the move to another — and if the win rate says yes, is the win rate telling the truth?
