## Teaching post-rigorously

My philosophy in developing course material is to connect revolutionary analogies and heuristics on one side with rigorous formalization and theorems on the other.

Mathematical education passes through a pre-rigorous stage worked in examples and intuitions, a rigorous stage worked formally, and a post-rigorous stage in which the two are held together and the intuition has been educated by the formalism. Stalling at the second stage, with intuition discarded as unrigorous, is the failure I design against. Advanced game theory and machine learning are taught mostly in that second register.

I applied this as a teaching assistant on the Demystifying AI course, where the design problem is to make a technical idea land for an audience without formal apparatus and without misrepresenting what the idea says. I exercise the same philosophy in my research: the work on how people manage several strategic relationships simultaneously, and the work on strategic dependence, both take an intuition practitioners already hold and formalize it far enough to be measured and argued with.

An intuition that has survived formalization is one that can be built on. It also gives students a basis for choosing a major and selecting courses that serve their own vision of themselves as citizens of a world changing this rapidly, with AI agents in it.

## Competitive cybersecurity learning

The capture-the-flag work is built for classroom use and not only for competition. A challenge set with a validated knowledge mapping can be taught, and once taught it can be varied: A/B testing across sections, with the sessions instrumented. I intend to submit the result as a NICE Framework success story, mapping the challenge set onto Work Roles and their Task, Knowledge and Skill statements, and reporting which statements students actually reached.

**The classroom informs the research.** Instrumented A/B testing supplies a dataset available nowhere else for the questions [Mental World Modeling](../overview/3-year-agenda/mental-world-modeling/) asks: what the algorithmic primitives of solving are, and how they evolve as a student gains experience. A competition records outcomes; a classroom records process and varies the conditions deliberately. The same record answers the question cybersecurity education needs answered, which is what students genuinely struggle with in penetration testing and security-operations work. That question carries more weight than it once did, because those are largely the problems AI agents also handle badly, and therefore the ones students cannot afford not to learn.

**The research informs the classroom.** A model of how people solve provides a principled basis for setting learning objectives, in place of an inherited list of topics, and for aligning those objectives with what the cybersecurity industry has become. It also determines what to put in front of students: in-class and take-home materials designed to reach the objective more effectively, in spite of AI and with AI. These are two distinct design problems and I treat them separately.
